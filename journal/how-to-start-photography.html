Basics · August 2026

How to start photography

The way to start photography is to use the camera you already own, on purpose, every day for a month. Gear can come later; the habit of seeing cannot, and the habit is the actual skill.

I started on disposable cameras at the zoo, convinced National Geographic would call any day. They did not, but the method held up, so here it is formalized.

Start with the camera in your pocket

Your phone is a better camera than the ones most working photographers learned on, and it removes every excuse. Shooting daily with intent, choosing the frame, waiting for the light, deleting the misses honestly, teaches more in a month than a new camera teaches in a year. The instrument is not the bottleneck yet. You are, and that is fixable, which is the good news hiding inside every beginner's frustration with their gear.

Learn one thing at a time

Pick a single skill per week and ignore the rest: light one week, composition the next, then motion, then editing. The basics post in this Journal covers the exposure triangle when you are ready for it. Trying to learn everything at once is how people end up with a camera bag full of guilt and an Instagram full of sunsets they cannot remember taking.

Shoot what is near you

Exotic locations are not required; repetition is. Pick somewhere five minutes away, mine was and still is a lake path, and shoot it in every light and season until you know it like a room in your house. Around Seattle, Green Lake, Lincoln Park, and any neighborhood street at golden hour will teach you everything: light, weather, timing, patience. The place matters less than returning to it.

When to buy a real camera

Buy when you hit a wall the phone cannot pass, usually background blur, low light, or reach, and buy used without fear: any mirrorless body from the last several years outperforms what professionals shot a decade ago. Spend the difference on one decent lens and, more usefully, on going places worth photographing. The camera is the cheapest part of every good photograph.

Volume finds your subject

You do not choose your photographic obsession; it surfaces. Shoot everything for six months, then look at what you kept: it will lean somewhere, people, animals, buildings, weather, and that lean is your answer. Mine surfaced at the zoo as a kid and never left; it just upgraded over the years from disposable cameras to long lenses and, eventually, to a safari where the alarm clock was actual lions.

The only rule I insist on

If it stops being fun, you are doing it wrong; the whole craft runs on curiosity, and grim discipline photographs poorly. Keep the fun and the rest compounds. And if watching a working session would help, book one and bring your questions, or just say hello here; encouraging beginners is a professional hobby of mine.
