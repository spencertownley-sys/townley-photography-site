What insurance and licensing you should expect from a professional photographer

A professional photographer should be able to show you three things without fumbling: liability insurance, any licenses their specific work requires, and a written agreement. Here is what each covers, and why asking about them is not rude, it is diligence.

Most clients never ask, because it feels awkward. The ones who do are usually planners, venues, and businesses, and they are right to; the answers separate professionals from hobbyists faster than any portfolio.

Liability insurance

General liability covers the scenarios nobody plans: a guest trips over a light stand, a tripod meets a venue floor the wrong way. Many Seattle-area venues will not allow a vendor on site without a certificate of insurance naming them, and a working professional will have sent dozens of those. If a photographer has never been asked for a COI, that tells you something about the rooms they have worked in.

Licensing: mostly about drones

Photography itself does not require a professional license in Washington beyond ordinary business registration. The sharp exception is aerial work: any paid drone photography legally requires an FAA Part 107 certificate, and much of the Seattle metro sits in controlled airspace requiring authorization per flight. I hold the Part 107 and handle those authorizations as routine. If aerials are part of your job, this is the one credential you should always verify, and a legitimate operator will volunteer the certificate number.

The written agreement

A contract is not bureaucracy; it is the list of promises with dates attached, and it protects both sides equally. It should name deliverables, turnaround, payment terms, the deposit and refund policy, and what happens when weather or illness intervenes. Mine states photos within 48 hours, video within a week, and a 25 percent deposit refundable with a week of notice, because a promise without a number is a mood.

The quiet fourth thing: backups

Not paperwork, but the same category of professionalism: dual card recording, duplicate backups of your files after the shoot, and gear redundancy on the day. Moments do not rerun, so ask how your photos are protected between the shutter and the delivery. A professional has a specific answer; an amateur has confidence.

Questions worth asking anyone

Can you send a certificate of insurance if my venue asks? Are you Part 107 certified, if aerials are involved? What exactly do I receive and when, in writing? What is the backup plan if you are sick or a camera fails? None of these offend a professional; all of them filter out the person whose business plan is optimism.

Why this matters more than it seems

Photography purchases are trust purchases: the wedding does not rerun, the listing launches once, the event happens today. Insurance, licensing, and contracts are how a stranger proves the trust is placed well before the day arrives. If you want any of the above in writing for your own booking, ask here; the plain answers are the point.
