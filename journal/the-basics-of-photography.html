The basics of photography

Photography comes down to controlling three things: how much light comes in, for how long, and how strongly the camera responds to it. Those are aperture, shutter speed, and ISO, and everything else in the menus is detail stacked on that triangle.

After ten-plus years of shooting professionally, this is how I explain the basics when someone actually wants to understand them, in the order that helps.

Light first, settings second

The most common beginner mistake is learning settings before learning to see light. Photographs are made of light, not megapixels: its direction, its softness, its color. An overcast Seattle sky is a giant soft light source, which is why phone photos here often look better than tourists expect. Before touching a dial, practice noticing where light comes from and what it does to a face. That skill outlasts every camera you will ever own.

Aperture: how much light, and what is sharp

Aperture is the size of the lens opening. Wider (smaller f-number, like f/2) lets in more light and blurs the background; narrower (f/8, f/11) keeps more of the scene sharp. That background blur is most of what people mean when a photo looks "professional," and it is a setting, not a secret; nothing about it requires expensive gear.

Shutter speed: how long the light pours in

Fast shutter speeds freeze motion, kids, dogs, birds; slow ones blur it, waterfalls, car lights, and also your own hand shake, which is where sharp photos quietly die. The working rule: match or beat your focal length (1/200th at 200mm), and go faster for anything that moves.

ISO: the volume knob

ISO turns up the camera's response when light runs out, at the price of grain. Modern cameras handle high ISO far better than beginners fear, and a grainy sharp photo beats a clean blurry one every single time. Raise it without guilt when the light demands it, and learn your own camera's ceiling once, on purpose, so the number stops being scary.

Composition: where things go

Start with three habits: put the subject off-center, check the edges of the frame for clutter, and get closer than feels polite. The rule of thirds is training wheels, useful until seeing balance becomes automatic. Simplicity carries more photographs than any technique; most weak images are just three photos fighting inside one frame. When a scene is not working, subtract from it before you add.

The order to learn it

Light, then aperture, then shutter, then ISO, then composition forever, because it never finishes. Resist learning them from menus; learn each one by breaking it on purpose for an afternoon and looking at what happened. Shoot in aperture priority while the triangle becomes instinct, and read the next post in this Journal on how to start photography for the practical path. And if you would rather watch the triangle handled at a relaxed pace, book a session and ask questions the whole time; I like the company.
