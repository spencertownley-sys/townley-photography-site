A day in the life: what an event videographer actually does on site

An event videographer's day starts long before the first guest arrives and ends well after teardown. Most of the job is invisible when it is done right, so here is the actual hour-by-hour, which is also an honest answer to what the fee pays for.

This is how I run event shoots around Seattle and Tacoma, from corporate programs to celebrations.

Before the day

The real work starts with the run-of-show. I map the schedule against the room: where the light comes from, where speakers stand, where the photographer will want to be, and where I can move without landing in anyone's photos. Event photo and video crews that plan together stay out of each other's frames all day, so I ask for the photographer's contact ahead of time. Key moments get a coverage plan; everything else gets a strategy. Venues I have not shot get scouted, in person or through a walkthrough call.

Load-in: audio before anything

On site early, and the first hour belongs to sound: lav mics for speakers, a recorder on the venue soundboard, sync tones, and backups for each. Then cameras, tripod positions, and a lighting read of the room as it will actually be, not as it looked empty. By doors-open, the technical decisions are made, which is what lets the rest of the day look calm.

During the event

Coverage is triage, and the run-of-show is the map. The scheduled moments, speeches, entrances, presentations, get locked-down cameras and full attention. Between them I hunt b-roll: hands, laughter, details of the room, the shots that make the edit feel like the day felt. The skill that matters most is anticipating, being set before the moment instead of chasing it, and staying out of the guests' experience while doing it. Guests should remember a camera existed only when they watch the film.

The parts nobody notices

Batteries rotate on a schedule. Cards get swapped before they fill and never leave my person. Everything records to dual cards, because event moments do not repeat, and the whole kit is built so one failure costs nothing. This paragraph is boring on purpose; so is the discipline, and it is a large part of what you are hiring.

After: the edit

Teardown is quick; the edit is not, and it is where the day becomes a film. Footage gets backed up twice before I sleep, then culled, synced with the audio recordings, color corrected, and cut to the deliverables we agreed on: full program, highlight film, social cutdowns, whatever the deliverables list says. Video projects run a one week turnaround, and the delivery date is part of the booking, not a hope.

The short version
