FAA Part 107, explained: why your real estate photographer needs a drone license

FAA Part 107 is the federal certificate that makes it legal to fly a drone for commercial work in the United States. If someone is being paid to photograph your listing from the air, they need it, no exceptions, and around Seattle the stakes are higher than most agents realize.

I hold a Part 107 certificate and fly DJI drones for listings across the Seattle and Tacoma area. Here is what the license actually involves and why you should care.

What the certificate covers

Part 107 requires passing a proctored FAA knowledge exam covering airspace classes, weather, flight restrictions, and operating rules, with recurrent online training every two years to keep the certificate current. It is the difference between a hobbyist who bought a drone and an operator the FAA holds to commercial standards: altitude limits, visual line of sight, no flights over people, and authorization before entering controlled airspace.

Why it matters here specifically

The Seattle metro is dense with controlled airspace: Sea-Tac, Boeing Field, Renton Municipal, and Paine Field between them cover a large share of the residential map. A legal flight over a listing in much of the metro area requires an airspace authorization before takeoff. A licensed operator handles that as routine paperwork, often approved in minutes through the FAA's automated system, sometimes requiring advance planning at lower altitudes near the fields. An unlicensed one either does not know the requirement exists or is hoping nobody notices, and neither is a person you want attached to your transaction.

What happens without it

Unlicensed commercial flights carry FAA fines that can reach five figures, and the exposure does not stop with the pilot. Photos from an illegal flight are baked into your listing and your brokerage's marketing, and insurers take a dim view of claims involving unauthorized flights. The cheap aerial add-on stops being cheap the moment anything goes wrong.

How to check

Ask the photographer directly for their Part 107 certificate number; a legitimate operator will not hesitate. You can also simply ask how they handle airspace authorization for your listing's neighborhood. If the answer is a blank look, book someone else. Any photographer flying legally will find the question easy, and most of us are glad an agent bothered to ask.

The other paperwork worth asking about

While you are asking questions, ask about liability insurance too. The license makes the flight legal; insurance covers the rare day physics wins anyway. A professional operator carries both and will say so plainly. Between the certificate, the authorization, and the coverage, the aerial photos on your listing should be the one part of the transaction with no fine print attached.
