What to wear for a family portrait session in the Pacific Northwest

The short answer: pick a soft, coordinated color palette, dress in layers, and let everyone look like themselves. In the Pacific Northwest, that means planning for clouds, wind, and damp grass more than for rain itself.

I have photographed enough families around Seattle to know that wardrobe stress ruins more sessions than weather does. So here is the advice I actually give clients, in the order I give it.

Start with a palette, not outfits

Have one person choose their outfit first, then build everyone else around it. Muted, earthy tones photograph beautifully against Northwest backdrops: cream, rust, olive, slate blue, camel, charcoal. You want colors that sit next to each other comfortably, not five people wearing the same shirt. The matching white-tops-and-jeans era is over, and nobody misses it.

Layers are the whole strategy

Our weather changes its mind hourly, and layers solve two problems at once. They keep everyone comfortable when a session starts in sun and ends in wind off the water, and they add texture, which is what makes photos in soft overcast light feel rich instead of flat. Knits, wool, corduroy, denim jackets, scarves: all of it works. A flat cotton t-shirt under gray skies is the hardest thing in my catalog to make interesting.

Think about your feet

Most of my favorite session spots, like Green Lake and Lincoln Park, involve grass, sand, or trail. Assume the ground is damp even in August, because it usually is. Boots look great and handle everything. Long hems collect mud. Brand-new white sneakers spend the whole session being protected instead of walked in.

What reads badly on camera

A few things consistently fight the photo: large logos and graphic text, neon colors that reflect onto skin, and very fine stripes or tight patterns, which can shimmer oddly on screens. Sunglasses parked on heads, phones in pockets, and smartwatches on a dressed-up wrist all end up in more frames than anyone expects. None of these are rules so much as things I quietly work around, and it is easier when I do not have to.

What about actual rain?

Light drizzle is workable and sometimes lovely, especially under tree cover, and a couple of umbrellas can become a prop instead of a problem. Real rain, the kind that soaks through a wool coat, is a reschedule, and I would rather move a session than deliver a gallery of people enduring weather. Either way, bring a towel and a change of shoes for the kids. Someone always finds the puddle, and the best frames often happen right after they do.

Comfort beats everything

My whole approach to sessions is simple: if we are not having fun, we are doing it wrong. The best images happen when everyone is relaxed, and nobody relaxes in an outfit they are worried about. Dress the kids in clothes they can move in, bring a snack, and let the session be a walk in the park with a camera along.
